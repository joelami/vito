# NFL Model Methodology

This document explains, in plain language, how the Phase 1 NFL model works, how it was tested, and what the testing actually found. It is written to be read start to finish, not skimmed. If you only read one section, read "Actual Results" — that's the honest bottom line on whether this thing is any good yet.

The short version, stated up front so nothing here reads as a reveal: the infrastructure is solid, the calibration is honest, and the model does not currently beat the market. Everything below explains why that's the conclusion and what would need to change for a different one.

## Data

The model runs on `Datasets/NFL/nfl.xlsx` — 5,431 games spanning the 2006 through 2025 seasons (the 2025 season runs through the February 2026 Super Bowl and is still partial as of this writing). Each game carries decimal odds for moneyline, spread, and totals markets, each captured at four points: the opening line, the minimum price seen, the maximum price seen, and the closing line.

The odds themselves come from three different sources over time: Pinnacle from 2014 through 2018, bet365 from 2018 through 2025, and Betr from September 2025 onward. This matters more than it might sound like — Pinnacle is widely regarded as one of the sharpest books in the market, meaning its lines are harder to beat, while bet365 and Betr are retail-facing books that may price more loosely. The model doesn't currently adjust for this difference; it treats every closing line the same regardless of which book set it. That's a real limitation and it's called out again below.

The other important wrinkle is coverage. Seasons 2006 through 2013 have opening-line data only — no closing line was captured for those years at all. That means those eight seasons are useful for building power-rating history (the Elo engine needs a long run-up to have any signal), but they can't be used for anything that depends on knowing where the market closed, which is most of the backtest. From 2014 forward, closing-line coverage is essentially complete, dipping to roughly 73-95% only in the still-in-progress 2025 season.

## Model Architecture

The model is three components stacked together: a power rating, a machine-learning layer, and an ensemble blend that combines them into a single probability per bet. Each is described on its own below.

### 1. Power Ratings

The power-rating engine (`core/power_ratings.py`) is an Elo-style system, the same family of rating used in chess and adapted for sports for decades. Every team starts at a neutral 1500. After each game, both teams' ratings move by an amount governed by three things: how big the win was, how surprising the win was, and a fixed learning rate (K=20).

Before comparing two teams, the home team's rating gets a home-field bonus of 48 Elo points added to it — roughly equivalent to 2.4 points of scoring margin — reflecting the general home-field advantage seen across the NFL. That bonus is then run through the standard logistic Elo formula to produce an expected result (essentially, a win probability) for the game.

After the game, the actual result is compared to that expectation, and the gap between them — scaled by a margin-of-victory multiplier — determines how far the rating moves. The multiplier is the standard Elo MOV formula, `ln(|margin|+1) * (2.2 / (winner_elo_diff*0.001 + 2.2))`, which does two things at once: it makes blowouts move ratings more than narrow wins, and it dampens that effect the more the winner was already expected to win. A 30-point win by a team that was already a heavy favorite moves the rating much less than the same margin from a team that was supposed to lose.

At the start of each new season, every team's rating regresses 33% of the way back toward the 1500 mean. This reflects a simple reality: rosters and coaching staffs turn over, and a team's form from last December isn't fully predictive of its form next September, so the model deliberately forgets some of what it learned.

One deliberate exception: neutral-site games — the Super Bowl and international games, 78 of the 5,431 total — get no home-field bonus at all, since the "home team" designation there is just a scheduling label, not an actual home crowd. This is worth flagging because it was a real bug caught during development: an earlier version of the model applied home-field advantage even in the Super Bowl, which handed a fictitious edge to whichever team happened to be labeled "home" in the data. It's fixed now, but it's a good example of the kind of quiet error that can sit in a model looking fine until someone checks for it specifically.

The single most important discipline in this whole component is that every prediction uses the rating as it stood strictly before that game — ratings update only after a game is final, never before it. This is what's usually called "walk-forward" evaluation, and it's what separates a real prediction from a lookback that's secretly using information it couldn't have had at the time. Without it, a backtest can look arbitrarily good and mean nothing.

**Calibration check.** The way to sanity-check a probability model isn't just "does it pick more winners than losers" — it's "when the model says 70%, does the thing happen 70% of the time." Bucketing the walk-forward Elo win probability into deciles and comparing against actual home-win rates produces a curve that tracks closely and monotonically: the 0.3-probability bucket sees an actual home win rate of roughly 0.29 to 0.36, the 0.7-plus bucket comes in around 0.77. In plain terms, the Elo engine's stated confidence levels are honest. It's not just directionally right about who's better — its probabilities mean what they claim to mean. That's a meaningful result on its own and it's the part of this system I'd trust most.

### 2. Machine Learning Leg

The second component (`core/ml_models.py`) is a pair of gradient-boosted trees — scikit-learn's `HistGradientBoostingRegressor` — one predicting the home team's scoring margin, the other predicting the game's total points.

The features going in are all schedule- and form-based: the rating differential from the Elo engine, rest-day differential and absolute rest days for each team, each team's trailing 10-game record against the spread, trailing win rate, trailing points scored and points allowed, current win/loss streak, and flags for divisional games, playoff games, and neutral-site games. One engineered feature does a lot of work here: a "naive implied score," where each team's expected points is just the average of its own scoring rate and its opponent's rate of allowing points. Simple as it is, this feature matters a lot for the totals prediction specifically.

The models are deliberately kept shallow and heavily regularized — max depth of 3, a minimum of 30 samples per leaf, 150 boosting iterations, a slow 0.05 learning rate. This isn't an oversight; it's a direct response to the size of the dataset. A few thousand rows of inherently noisy game outcomes is exactly the kind of data a flexible model overfits quickly, memorizing noise instead of learning signal. Keeping the trees shallow is a bet on generalization over fit.

Training uses an expanding window by season: to generate predictions for season Y, the model is fit only on seasons strictly before Y, then the window slides forward one season and refits before predicting the next one. A season is never present in its own training data, and no future season can leak backward into an earlier one's predictions. This is the same walk-forward discipline as the Elo engine, applied to the ML leg.

Measured out-of-sample, starting with the 2009 season (the first season with at least three prior seasons available to train on): the correlation between predicted margin and actual margin is about 0.36. For comparison, the rating differential alone — no ML at all — gets about 0.38. In other words, the ML leg is roughly on par with the power rating for predicting margin, not a clear improvement on it. It adds a second, differently-built opinion more than it adds raw accuracy.

Total points prediction is the weak point of this whole model, and it's worth stating plainly rather than softening it. The ML model's predicted total correlates with the actual total at only about 0.17 — and the naive scoring-pace baseline described above, on its own, actually does slightly better, at about 0.21. Total points in the NFL are genuinely hard to predict from team-strength and pace features alone. This model has no injury data, no weather data, and no pace-of-play or personnel information, and those are the real drivers of how many points a given game produces. The totals leg of this model should be trusted less than the margin leg, and the results below bear that out directly.

### 3. Ensemble Blending

The blending step (`core/ensemble.py`) takes each submodel's raw point prediction — a margin or a total — and converts it into a probability of clearing a specific betting line, using a normal-CDF approximation. The spread of error used for that conversion isn't guessed at; it's measured directly from the walk-forward run itself. Margin prediction residuals have a standard deviation of about 13.6 points for both the Elo and ML legs — they're nearly identical in how wrong they tend to be, on average — and total-points residuals run about 13.7 to 13.8 points.

For moneyline and spread bets, the two probabilities being blended are the Elo-based one and the ML-based one. For totals, it's the ML-based probability and the naive-baseline-based probability, since the Elo rating has no opinion on scoring pace at all. The two probabilities are averaged 50/50 into a single final "model probability" for the bet.

Alongside that probability, a confidence tier — High, Medium, or Low — gets assigned based on how much the two submodels agree with each other, not on how large the resulting edge looks. If both models favor the same side and their probabilities are within 5 points of each other, that's High confidence. Within 12 points, Medium. Beyond that, or if the two models actually disagree about which side to favor at all, it's Low — regardless of how big the blended edge appears. This is a deliberate design choice: a large edge built on two models that don't agree with each other is treated as the least trustworthy situation, not the most exciting one. A big number produced by disagreement is more likely to be noise than a big number produced by consensus.

## Edge-Finding and Staking

Betting markets quote two-sided prices — both sides of a moneyline, both sides of a spread, over and under on a total — and both sides always add up to more than 100% implied probability. That extra is the vig, the book's built-in margin. Before comparing the model's opinion to the market's, that vig has to be stripped out. `core/odds_math.py` does this with the standard multiplicative method: take the raw implied probabilities from both sides and normalize them so they sum to exactly 1.0, producing the market's fair, vig-free probability.

`core/edge_finder.py` then compares the model's probability to that fair market probability. Edge percentage is simply the difference between them, expressed as a percentage point gap. From there, expected value and a bet size are computed using fractional Kelly staking, at a default of quarter-Kelly. Full Kelly sizing is mathematically optimal only if the win probability being staked is exactly correct — and no model's probability is ever exactly correct. Sizing at quarter-Kelly is a hedge against the model's own uncertainty about itself; it costs some growth rate if the model happens to be right, in exchange for a much smaller loss if it's wrong.

Closing-line value, or CLV, is tracked throughout as the more reliable long-run signal — more reliable than whether any individual bet won or lost. Win/loss on a single game is dominated by variance; a good bet loses all the time and a bad bet wins plenty. CLV measures whether the price taken was better than where the market ultimately settled, which is a much lower-variance signal of whether the underlying read on the game was actually sound.

## Backtest Methodology — The Credibility Check

This is the part of the system most worth understanding in detail, because it's the difference between a backtest that means something and one that doesn't.

Every bet in the backtest (`core/backtest.py`) is priced at the game's opening line, never the closing line. This sounds like a small detail but it isn't. A closing line, by definition, is only known once betting on that game has closed — you can never actually place a bet at a price that doesn't exist yet. Pricing every backtested bet at open is what a bettor could actually have gotten in real time, on the day the model made its call. Closing-line value is then measured separately, comparing the price that was actually taken against where the market ended up moving by kickoff.

This is the single most important design choice in the whole system. It's built to answer "would this have actually worked, betting into real prices as they existed," not "does this look good when compared against a price that hadn't happened yet." A huge amount of bad backtesting in this space comes from exactly that mistake — evaluating against closing lines as if they were available at bet time, which flatters the model with information it never actually had.

## Actual Results

This is the centerpiece of the document, so the numbers are reported exactly as the pipeline produced them, with no rounding in the model's favor.

Running the full walk-forward backtest from the 2009 season onward, at a 3%-or-greater edge threshold restricted to Medium or High confidence bets, with quarter-Kelly staking, the pipeline surfaced 6,125 qualifying bets.

**Overall:** 6,125 bets, a 46.4% hit rate, an ROI of -0.78%, average CLV of -0.30%, and only 29.9% of bets achieved positive closing-line value — meaning more than two-thirds of the time, the line moved against the price this model would have taken.

**By market:**

- Moneyline (2,468 bets): 38.7% hit rate. This is expected to run under 50% on its own, since moneyline bets with edge tend to skew toward underdogs — a lower hit rate on underdog-heavy bets isn't damning by itself, and needs to be read alongside ROI and CLV rather than in isolation.
- Spread (1,671 bets): 51.6% hit rate.
- Total (1,986 bets): 51.9% hit rate.

**CLV-positive rate by market:** moneyline 34.0%, spread 34.1%, total 21.3%. Totals are the weakest by a wide margin here, which lines up with the weak total-prediction correlation documented in the ML section above — the model's read on total points is the least reliable part of it, and the CLV numbers confirm that independently.

**Parameter sweep.** A sweep across edge thresholds of 3%, 5%, 8%, 10%, and 15%, restricted to High-confidence bets only, produced ROI point estimates that bounced between roughly -4% and +10% depending on the specific market and threshold. On its face, some of those numbers look promising. They aren't reliable. Every one of these ROI estimates carries a standard error roughly as large as, or larger than, the estimate itself. The clearest example: spread bets at a 15%-plus edge threshold, High confidence only, produced 120 bets at an ROI of +10.35% — with a standard error of plus or minus 8.7%. That range comfortably includes zero. It is not a statistically distinguishable result from "no edge at all." No market, threshold, or confidence combination tested in this sweep shows a robust, persistent edge that survives its own uncertainty band.

**The conclusion, stated directly:** this first-pass model has not found a demonstrated, statistically credible edge against the closing line. That is not the same as saying the project failed. The infrastructure — walk-forward integrity with no future leakage, honest open-priced backtesting, CLV tracking, and a calibration check that came back genuinely well-behaved — is sound, and building that infrastructure correctly is the actually valuable output of this phase. But the specific model tested here, an Elo power rating blended with a shallow gradient-boosted model on schedule and form features, does not currently beat the market.

That's the expected outcome for a first pass with no injury data, no weather data, no roster data, and no line-movement data, tested against sportsbooks that include Pinnacle — one of the sharpest, hardest-to-beat books in the industry — for a meaningful chunk of the sample period. If real, exploitable edge exists, finding it will likely require one or more of: richer inputs (injury reports, weather, referee tendencies, line-movement velocity or steam detection), narrower and more specific signals rather than a general power-rating-plus-ML blend (situational systems like specific rest/travel spots, backup-quarterback situations, or division-rivalry unders), or shopping multiple books for the best available price rather than assuming a single book's line is the one to beat. None of those are implemented in this phase.

## Known Limitations

Stated explicitly rather than left implicit:

- **No injury, weather, roster, or coaching-change data.** This is the single biggest gap in the model and the most likely reason total-points prediction in particular is weak.
- **2006-2013 seasons have no closing-line data.** They're included in power-rating history, since Elo benefits from a long run-up, but excluded from anything CLV-related, since there's no closing price to measure against.
- **Franchise relocations and rebrands** — Oakland to Las Vegas Raiders, San Diego to LA Chargers, St. Louis to LA Rams, and Washington's three franchise names — are treated as one continuous team for rating purposes. That's a modeling convenience, not a fact; rosters and coaching staffs don't carry over cleanly through a relocation, and the rating history assumes more continuity than actually existed on the field.
- **Ties are not modeled as a distinct outcome** in a few places in the pipeline. Ties are extremely rare under current NFL overtime rules, so the practical impact is negligible, but it hasn't been rigorously handled.
- **The sportsbook source changes over time** — Pinnacle, then bet365, then Betr. Per-row devigging accounts for the specific vig level of whichever book priced that game, but the model makes no adjustment for the fact that a professionally sharp book like Pinnacle and retail-facing books like bet365 and Betr behave differently in how exploitable their lines actually are. A backtest that blends across all three is blending across meaningfully different opponents.
- **Backtested ROI, even where positive, is not a guarantee of anything going forward.** Markets adapt, and small-sample positive results are fully consistent with noise — see the standard-error discussion in the Actual Results section, which applies to any future re-run of this sweep just as much as it applies to this one.
- **The 2025 season is partial** — through the February 2026 Super Bowl at time of writing, with closing-line coverage still dipping to 73-95% for parts of it since it's the newest data in the set. Any results specific to that season should be treated as provisional, not final.

## What Would Change This Conclusion

The backtest here is necessarily retrospective — it's asking whether this model would have worked in the past, against prices that already happened. It's a credibility check, not a proof either way about the future. The honest next test, once this model starts generating real picks against the 2026 season as it happens, is forward-tested performance, and it should be held to a higher bar than the backtest, not a lower one.

What would actually indicate a real edge:

- **Sustained positive CLV over a genuinely large forward-tested sample** — not a good month, and not a cherry-picked market or threshold, but positive average CLV tracked across every bet placed, over a sample large enough that the standard error is meaningfully smaller than the average, the same discipline that ruled out the backtest's parameter sweep above.
- **A CLV-positive rate that clears roughly 50%,** consistently, rather than the 29.9% seen in this backtest. Beating the closing line more often than not, over time, is a much stronger signal than any single stretch of winning bets.
- **Consistency across markets and time periods**, not a result driven by one hot streak in one market. If spread bets are forward-profitable but totals remain weak, that would actually corroborate this report's finding that the totals leg is the weakest link — a coherent story rather than a lucky streak.
- **Results holding up after any model change**, not just once. If a future version adds injury or weather data and shows improvement, that improvement needs its own walk-forward and forward-test validation before being trusted — the same standard applied here, applied again.

Until forward-tested results clear that bar, the right posture toward this model is the one this document has taken throughout: useful as infrastructure, not yet demonstrated as an edge.

## Phase 2: Live Data, the Forward-Test Harness, and NCAAF

This section covers what changed after the above was written, in the same session, in response to three requests: pull in real current-game data, build something that continuously evaluates and improves the model, and expand to the newly-added datasets.

### Live data: ESPN's public scoreboard feed

Games and odds now sync automatically from ESPN's public (unauthenticated, undocumented) scoreboard endpoint — no paid API, no signup. It turned out to embed real DraftKings odds (moneyline, spread, total, each with an open AND a close snapshot) directly on every event, which is a strict upgrade over the schedule-only plan originally scoped: it removes manual odds entry as the default path while staying at zero cost. Because the endpoint is undocumented, `core/espn_client.py` treats it defensively (every field access degrades to "missing" rather than crashing) and manual entry remains as a fallback for props or anything ESPN doesn't carry. One real gotcha found and fixed during integration: the endpoint 403s any custom `User-Agent` header (browser-style or not) but allows an HTTP client's own unmodified default — a quirk of ESPN's edge WAF, not documented anywhere, worth remembering if this ever needs debugging again.

A second, more important thing was checked before any of this touched the model: **NFL preseason games are explicitly excluded.** The historical dataset this model trained on contains regular-season and postseason games only — confirmed by season row counts — so a preseason game (backups playing, different incentives, no historical analog) is filtered out via ESPN's own `season.type` field before it ever reaches the model. This mattered immediately, since the app was built during NFL preseason itself; without the filter, the very first live games synced would have been scored by a model that had never seen anything like them.

### The forward-test harness

`backend/harness.py`, run daily via a `launchd` job (see `scripts/run_harness.sh`), is the "constantly evaluate and get better" loop. Each run: syncs real games/odds, scores every unsettled one at the **opening** price (never the closing price — the same discipline the backtest uses, since a bettor can never actually transact at a price that only existed after the fact), logs every qualifying edge into a `forward_picks` table, and settles any picks whose game has since finished using the exact same win/loss/push logic as the historical backtest (`settle_bet`, shared between the two so they can't quietly diverge). This produces a genuine forward track record — what the model would have done, for real, going forward — which is a fundamentally stronger signal than the backtest, for the reason the "What Would Change This Conclusion" section above already laid out: it can't be retroactively curve-fit.

Both the live app and the harness now build the model through one shared function, `pipeline.py`'s `build_nfl_pipeline()` — before this refactor, the app and an offline job would have needed to duplicate that sequence, which is exactly how the two quietly drift apart over time. A synthetic end-to-end test (a fake regular-season game pushed through the full snapshot → settle → CLV cycle) caught a real bug before it could reach production: ESPN's dates are timezone-aware, the historical dataset's dates are not, and subtracting the two crashed. Since it's currently NFL preseason, there was no real regular-season game available yet to test this path against — the synthetic test existed specifically to avoid shipping an untested code path and finding out weeks from now, once the season starts, that it was broken.

### A real, honestly-measured model change: Pythagorean win expectation

Prompted by a quick review of a public NFL betting-model writeup, one new feature was added: each team's Pythagorean expected win percentage (points-for and points-against raised to the 2.37 exponent, the standard NFL adaptation of the sabermetric original), computed from the same walk-forward-safe rolling scoring data already in the feature set. This is exactly the kind of change the "Results holding up after any model change" bar above demands be measured, not assumed — so it was: margin correlation moved from 0.364 to 0.362, total correlation from 0.173 to 0.173, backtest ROI from -0.78% to -0.57%, all within noise of each other. **The feature made no measurable difference.** It's kept in — it's well-motivated, costs nothing, and doesn't hurt — but reported as a null result rather than dressed up as an improvement, because that's the standard this whole project is built on. The same review also surfaced a validated, not-yet-implemented next step: weather (temperature, wind, dome/outdoor) is a real predictive feature for totals in published work, this dataset doesn't have it, and the newly-added CFB dataset does — a natural place to test it first.

### NCAAF: built, validated, and honestly inconclusive

A full NCAAF module (`backend/sports/cfb/`) was built reusing the same core engine unchanged, and independently re-verified rather than taken on faith. The rating engine itself calibrates well after tuning to college football's larger scoring variance (k=40, home-field=90 Elo points, vs. the NFL's 20/48), and the ML leg beats a pure-Elo baseline on margin the same way it does for NFL. But the market side of the data undermines any confident conclusion: odds coverage is sparse and concentrated in a roughly 2012-2022 window rather than spanning the full 2004-2025 history, spread/total pricing is a single assumed -110/-110 snapshot with no real two-sided price (making CLV meaningless for this dataset specifically), and no market segment showed a statistically distinguishable edge — 2,113 backtested bets, -0.58% ROI ± 2.53% standard error overall, every by-market and by-season breakdown swallowed by its own noise band. The honest read: this data doesn't support a credible CFB model yet, and isn't rich enough to rule an edge in or out with any confidence either way. NCAAF is not wired into the live app or harness yet — that's a deliberate scope decision, not an oversight, pending either better odds coverage or a real CLV-capable dataset. A concrete candidate for closing that gap: **CollegeFootballData.com (CFBD)**, a free (API-key-gated) REST API that exposes betting lines alongside SP+, FPI, and EPA/PPA advanced metrics — richer than the current CSV on both the market side and the feature side. Not yet integrated; a genuine next step, not a promise.

### 2026-08-11: CFB rankings and week-over-week trends

Prompted directly by the app owner's question — "maybe team rankings largely don't matter in college football" plus a request to look at week-over-week trends. Two scoped hypotheses, tested via `sports/cfb/research_week_trends.py` following `core/research.py`'s discipline exactly, both re-verified independently against `sports/cfb/verify.py` before being reported.

**A) AP/CFP ranking data vs. Elo.** Checked the raw dataset directly rather than assuming: `Datasets/College Football/cfb-games.csv`'s full 30-column list, and the header row of all three box-score files alongside it, contain no AP poll rank, CFP rank, coaches' poll, or seed column of any kind — nothing rank-shaped anywhere in this data. Per the task's own instruction, no external ranking source was substituted or fabricated as a stand-in. **This half of the question is not testable on this dataset.** Not "inconclusive" — there is no ranking column to build a hypothesis from, so nothing was run through `evaluate_hypothesis()` for this half; the honest finding is that the input simply doesn't exist here.

**B) Week-over-season trends.** A real diagnostic first: the production model's own out-of-sample fit, split by phase of season using the real `week` column, showed genuine dispersion — margin correlation ran about 0.38 in weeks 1-3, dropped to about 0.27 for weeks 4 through 16, and fell further to about 0.13 in bowl/postseason games, even though `is_bowl` and `rest_days` were already available features. One data wrinkle mattered here and is worth recording: this dataset labels **every** bowl/postseason game `week=1` (a `season_type`-driven placeholder, not a real in-season week number — verified directly, 599 out of 599 `is_bowl` rows have `week==1`), so bowls had to be broken out as their own bucket rather than folded into "early season" where the raw column would have wrongly placed them.

That pattern motivated one concrete, well-reasoned hypothesis: **`season_week_adj`**, a calendar-correct season-phase feature — `week` as-is for regular-season games, with bowl games reassigned to that season's max regular-season week + 1 so they sort chronologically after the real regular season instead of looking like week-1 openers. Measured against the then-current production feature set: margin_corr 0.3589 → 0.3778 (+0.0190, well above the project's 0.005 noise floor), total_corr 0.1556 → 0.1566 (+0.0010, essentially flat), backtest ROI -3.05% → -2.38% (+0.67pp, well within its own ±2.53pp standard error, and not the "ROI up with no fit basis" pattern the framework flags as suspicious). `evaluate_hypothesis()`'s own recommendation: **adopt** (a real, non-suspicious statistical-fit improvement that did not hurt ROI). Logged in `decision_log.jsonl` as `cfb_season_week_adj`.

This is now permanent: `season_week_adjusted()` lives in `sports/cfb/features.py`, `build_features()` computes it as `season_week_adj`, and it's included in the live `ML_FEATURE_COLS`. Re-verified independently afterward by re-running `sports/cfb/verify.py` (a script with no dependency on the research script that produced the original numbers) — it reproduced margin_corr 0.3778, total_corr 0.1566, 2,160 qualifying bets, ROI -2.381% ± 2.501pp exactly.

Two other angles were checked during the same pass and explicitly not pursued further, in the same spirit of reporting a real null result rather than manufacturing a positive one: a "games played this season so far" feature was rejected before it was even tested, because this dataset holds only ~150-200 games per season leaguewide against a real FBS season's ~780 — a sparse sample, not a full schedule capture, so a count derived from it would mostly measure how sparsely a team happened to be sampled rather than genuine season progress. A short-rest-vs-normal-rest split showed an apparent fit improvement on short rest, but on only ~150 of 3,281 games (almost entirely Tuesday/Wednesday MACtion-style games) — exactly the kind of small-sample swing this project's own noise-floor discipline exists to catch, so it wasn't built into its own hypothesis.

### MLB, NBA, NHL, and player props: scoped, not built

Several more things came up this session and were deliberately NOT rushed into the same pass:

- **MLB, NBA, and NHL data** added to `Datasets/` all share the same shape of problem: real, rich, game- or player-level performance data with **zero odds data anywhere in any of them**. MLB is Retrosheet game logs and event files (1990-2025, final scores only). NBA is 849,680 rows of player-level box scores (points, rebounds, assists, etc. per player per game — score is embedded as a string like "108-89" and would need parsing into a team-level result). NHL is the same MoneyPuck player-stats data already powering the separate Hockey Scout App (skater-level, not game-result-level). All three are genuinely useful for building a power-rating engine — none of them have anything to backtest edge against without a separate odds source. The path forward for each is either a historical odds dataset (aussportsbetting.com, the source behind the NFL data, publishes similar spreadsheets for other leagues) or building ratings now and relying on ESPN's live odds feed once each season is live — `core/espn_client.py` already has NBA, NHL, and MLB scoreboard paths stubbed alongside NFL and CFB, unverified but following the identical pattern already proven out for NFL. A public NBA project (kyleskom/NBA-Machine-Learning-Sports-Betting) names **SBR (Sports Book Review)** as a source it scrapes historical multi-book odds from specifically for NBA — a concrete lead worth validating (reachability, ToS, data quality) before building against it, not yet done.
- **Player props** need fundamentally different infrastructure than everything built so far: player-level historical performance data (the NBA/NHL data above is actually a head start here, unlike the NFL/CFB team-level odds data), a source for actual per-player prop lines (not something ESPN's scoreboard feed carries), and a different modeling approach (player projections, not team power ratings). Worth doing, not something to bolt onto the current architecture without its own scoping pass.

## 2026-08-11: NBA player-venue research — "does a star's home/road split predict the TEAM's?" (honest null)

The app owner asked whether certain players historically perform worse in certain venues, in a way that could feed the TEAM-level moneyline/spread/total models (still no player-prop infrastructure — this stayed scoped to a team-level feature, per `sports/nba/research_star_venue_split.py`). NBA was chosen over NHL for this pass: `Datasets/NBA/nba-box-scores.csv` is a single, manageable 118MB file with a plain `home_away` flag, per-player minutes and points, and an already-proven join methodology into the live NBA game data (`sports/nba/research_four_factors.py`'s nearest-date/team-pair join, 90.6% coverage). NHL's equivalent MoneyPuck skater files are multiple gigabytes with their own unverified game-id join — a noisier starting point for the same question.

**Exploration (before formalizing anything):** a team's "star" was defined as the prior season's minutes leader for that franchise — fixed before the season starts, so identifying the star never uses the same-season data being tested (fully walk-forward safe, unlike picking "this season's leading scorer," which would be circular). Five independent operationalizations of "does the star's personal home-road split predict the team's home-road split" were checked directly against `sports/nba/loader.py`'s own game results at the team-season level (n≈443-449 team-seasons):

1. Star's career-to-date PPG home-road gap vs. team's actual home-road scoring-margin gap: **r = -0.0270, p = 0.5712**.
2. Same check with lookahead allowed (same-season, in-sample, the most generous possible version): **r = -0.0162, p = 0.7351**.
3. Star's plus-minus home-road gap instead of PPG: **r = -0.1128, p = 0.0175** — clears p<0.05 in isolation, but wrong-signed relative to the hypothesis, doesn't replicate on the same players using PPG, and plus-minus is a well-documented high-variance metric. Treated as noise, not a finding — exactly the multiple-comparisons trap this project's own Bonferroni logic exists to catch, surfaced by simply running five checks before ever calling `evaluate_hypothesis`.
4. Top-3-minutes players, minute-weighted PPG gap (testing whether "the star" alone is too narrow): **r = -0.0174, p = 0.7141**.
5. Star's PPG gap vs. the team's home-road **win-percentage** gap instead of scoring margin: **r = -0.0003, p = 0.9956**.

Four of five independent checks land on essentially zero; the one exception is weak, wrong-signed, and non-replicating. This is a clean null at the exploration stage.

**Formal test anyway.** Per this project's standing practice (the NHL turnover-differential and NBA offensive-rebound-rate hypotheses were both formally tested and rejected rather than skipped once exploration looked weak), a real `Hypothesis` (`star_player_home_road_venue_split`) was built and run through the actual walk-forward pipeline via `evaluate_hypothesis()` — a walk-forward-safe, game-level `home_star_venue_gap`/`away_star_venue_gap` feature (each identified star's own expanding home-PPG-minus-away-PPG split, using only that player's games strictly before the game being scored) was added to `ML_FEATURE_COLS` and measured:

- margin_corr: 0.405157 → 0.405126 (**-0.00003**, far inside the 0.005 noise floor)
- total_corr: 0.632622 → 0.632539 (**-0.00008**, likewise)
- ROI: -2.702% → -2.685% (**+0.017pp**, baseline stderr ±0.86pp — trivially within noise)
- `recommendation`: **"adopt_cautiously"** (the standard label for a harmless, well-motivated null, not a sign of any real gain)

Independently re-verified by re-running `python -m sports.nba.verify` from a clean state: its own reported margin_corr (0.4052), total_corr (0.6326), bet count (22,354), and ROI (-2.702%, stderr 0.861%) match the research script's baseline numbers exactly, confirming the live pipeline was measured correctly and left untouched.

**Not adopted, despite clearing the guardrail.** `evaluate_hypothesis()`'s "adopt or adopt_cautiously" bar is a necessary gate, not a mandate to keep everything that clears it. This case is judged differently from NFL's Pythagorean feature (also an "adopt_cautiously" null, kept in production): Pythagorean win expectation costs nothing to keep — it's computed purely from scoring history already flowing through the pipeline. This feature would cost something real: wiring the live NBA pipeline to load and join a 118MB player-level box-score file on every run, for a feature that's neutral-filled for 67.8% of games (only 19,033/59,093 games had an identified, sufficiently-sampled star) and demonstrably moves nothing. `sports/nba/config.py`, `features.py`, and `loader.py` were left exactly as found. `sports/nba/research_star_venue_split.py` and the `decision_log.jsonl` entry (`Hypothesis test: star_player_home_road_venue_split`) are the permanent record.

## Phase 3: Multi-Sport Live Harness, Suggestions of the Day, and Parlay

Everything in Phase 2 (ESPN sync, the forward-test harness, `pipeline.py`'s shared build function) was built and tested against NFL only, with a generic `pipeline.build_pipeline(sport)` / `core/matchup.py` dispatcher added for MLB/NBA/NHL but never actually wired into the live harness or app. This phase closed that gap end-to-end and surfaced two real bugs in the process — both found by actually running the multi-sport path against live data, not by code review.

### `core/dispatch.py`: one place that decides NFL-dedicated vs. generic

`harness.py` (the offline cron job) and `main.py` (the live app) each need to answer the same question per sport — "does this go through NFL's dedicated matchup scorer (weather, Pythagorean features) or the generic multi-sport one?" That decision now lives in exactly one place, `core/dispatch.py`, imported by both — the same "one shared source of truth" principle `pipeline.py`'s own docstring argues for, applied one level up so the two callers can't quietly disagree about which sport uses which scorer.

### Two real bugs found by actually running it against live MLB data (in-season)

1. **A SQL placeholder count bug** in `harness.py`'s `espn_games` upsert (27 named columns, only 25 `?` placeholders in `VALUES`) meant every non-NFL sync failed immediately on first contact — never caught before because the harness had only ever been run against NFL, which was in its off-season the whole time this bug existed.
2. **No ESPN-display-name resolution for MLB/NBA/NHL.** `core/matchup.py` resolves a team via `config.canonical_franchise(display_name)`, but only NFL had that function — MLB's `canonical_team()` expects a Retrosheet code, NBA had no canonical function at all (would have raised `AttributeError` on first live NBA game), and NHL's `canonical_team_id()` expects its numeric id. Left alone, this would have crashed on NBA/NHL and, worse, silently defaulted every MLB game's rating to the flat starting value (1500 for both teams) — a pick that *looks* like a real model output but carries zero actual signal. Fixed by adding a real `canonical_franchise(espn_display_name)` to each sport's `config.py`, built from the actual codes/team_ids each sport's own loaded dataset uses (not guessed) — e.g. MLB's `ESPN_NAME_TO_CODE = {"New York Yankees": "NYA", ...}`.

Verified end-to-end against real, live ESPN data (MLB, in-season): synced 100 real games, logged 24 genuine forward picks, and spot-checked that team ratings resolved to real, non-default values (Yankees/Dodgers checked directly) rather than the flat-1500 failure mode bug #2 would otherwise have produced silently.

### A third bug found only after picks started flowing through a UI: NULL-line duplicate picks

`forward_picks` had `UNIQUE(sport, espn_event_id, market, side, line)` to make `harness.py`'s `INSERT OR IGNORE` idempotent across repeated runs. Standard SQL NULL semantics broke this silently: `line` is always NULL for moneyline picks, and NULL is never considered equal to NULL for uniqueness purposes — so two rows both having `line IS NULL` were never treated as a collision, and every harness run re-logged every still-open moneyline pick as a brand new row. Found by noticing an actual duplicate pick (same game/market/side, two different `snapshotted_at` timestamps) in the live `/api/suggestions/daily` response — not hypothesized from reading the schema. Fixed with a `COALESCE(line, -999999)`-based unique index (SQLite's standard fix for "NULL should behave as equal to itself" in a uniqueness check), plus a one-time migration that deduped the rows the bug had already produced (24 real rows survived from 32). This matters beyond tidiness: an uncaught version of this bug would have double- (or triple-, quadruple-...) counted the same real-world outcome in the Live Track Record's ROI/hit-rate numbers once those picks settled — corrupting the one number this whole project treats as the actual truth.

### Suggestions of the Day and the Parlay tab

`/api/suggestions/daily` reads (never re-scores) whatever the harness has already logged as a qualifying, unsettled `forward_picks` row across every live sport, grouped by league — the model already decided what qualifies, at the honest opening price, in `harness.py`'s `snapshot_new_picks`; this route's only job is to read and group it. It also runs `core/parlay.py`'s `suggest_parlays()` over that same pool to surface ranked, cross-game-only parlay candidates (same-game legs are excluded from the pool entirely, not just flagged, per that module's existing correlation-honesty rule). The frontend's new **Suggestions** tab (now the app's landing page) and **Parlay** tab (suggested parlays + a manual leg-picker wired to the existing `/api/parlay/combine`) both consume this one endpoint, so they can never show a different picture of "what's live right now."

`/api/games/upcoming?sport=...` had a latent bug of its own worth noting here: it always scored games through the NFL pipeline/scorer regardless of the requested `sport`, because `_data` was NFL-only before this phase. Fixed as part of the same pass — the route now dispatches through `core/dispatch.py` against a real per-sport pipeline (`main.py`'s startup now builds all four live sports' pipelines, not just NFL's).

### Model introspection: `compute_feature_importance()`

Added to `core/ml_models.py` — sklearn permutation importance (shuffle one feature, measure how much worse the model gets, averaged over repeats) run against the final fitted margin/total models. Not a hypothesis test (there's no baseline/variant here, it's introspection on the model as it exists), but the same honest spirit as `core/research.py`: telling you what to look into next from real evidence. Spot-checked against MLB: `rating_diff_pre` dominates margin prediction as expected; for totals, the starting-pitcher rolling-ER feature and the naive pace baseline dominate — both a sensible match for what should actually drive baseball scoring. Not yet wired into an API route/UI (permutation importance is expensive — refits a model and runs N shuffles — so it wasn't added to the eager pipeline-build path; it needs its own lazy-fetched route, the same pattern already used for QB injury status).

### Player-availability flags beyond QB/NFL

Extended the same "context flag, not a model input" pattern QB status already used (`sports/nfl/injuries.py`) to MLB, and found a genuinely better data source in the process: ESPN's scoreboard embeds each competitor's announced probable starting pitcher directly (`competitor.probables[0]`, with season ERA/W-L) on the exact same call `harness.sync_espn_games` already makes — no extra roster/depth-chart requests needed at all, unlike the two-call-per-team QB flag. `core/espn_client.py`'s new `parse_probable_pitchers()` + `sports/mlb/probables.py` + `/api/mlb/probable-pitchers` (lazy-fetched, 1hr cache, same pattern as QB status) surface this, and it's now wired into the Suggestions page under each MLB matchup.

Also found and fixed: the QB-status route (`/api/injuries/qb-status`) had existed since Phase 2 but was never actually called from the frontend — a real, orphaned feature. Wired into the Dashboard's synced-games cards now.

**NHL goalie status was investigated and explicitly deferred, not built.** The natural equivalent (starting goalie is highly predictive in hockey betting) hit a real wall: ESPN's depth-chart endpoint returns no `depthchart` key at all for NHL (confirmed empty across three different teams' live requests), and roster-listing order — the QB flag's original, since-replaced fallback — was independently reconfirmed unreliable here too (Boston's actual starter, Jeremy Swayman, listed 3rd in roster order, not 1st). Shipping a goalie flag keyed off a signal already proven unreliable for this exact purpose would repeat a known mistake knowingly. No fallback data source (e.g. games-started stats) was readily available without materially more API load, and NHL is off-season anyway, so there's no live game to validate against regardless. Left undone on purpose, not silently skipped.

**NBA was not attempted this pass.** Unlike QB or starting goalie, there's no single obviously-critical position to key an availability flag off — a "the team's star is out" flag would need a per-team-per-season "who's the star" determination (the same kind of judgment call the earlier player-venue research made for a different feature) rather than a fixed position lookup, and NBA is also off-season right now. Scoped as a real next step, not built.

One more real bug found while testing this pass, unrelated to the availability flags themselves but caught by the same end-to-end verification discipline: `x-show="g._best"` on the Dashboard's game cards didn't actually protect the nested `g._best.confidence`/`.market`/`.edge_pct` bindings from being evaluated when `_best` was `null` — Alpine.js only toggles CSS display with `x-show`, it still evaluates every binding on an element regardless of visibility. Any real synced game with zero qualifying opportunities (the normal case for most games) would have thrown console errors in production. Fixed by switching to `<template x-if="g._best">`, which actually removes the element (and stops evaluating its bindings) when the condition is false — verified via a synthetic in-browser test injecting both a no-opportunities game and a has-opportunities game side by side, since no real NFL game exists to test against right now (off-season).

## 2026-08-12: NHL re-verification, and two new special-teams/goaltending hypotheses

Prompted by the app owner's request to re-run and try to improve every model. Scope: NHL only. First, the current production baseline was re-verified honestly rather than assumed from this document's earlier numbers (`python -m sports.nhl.verify`, fresh run): **margin_corr 0.14591, total_corr 0.07502, 10,610 qualifying bets, ROI +13.323% ± 0.926% stderr.** This is NOT the 15,106-bet/+15.56% figure this document originally recorded for the NHL baseline — that earlier number predates the shot-differential/PP-conversion adoption documented in `features.py`; the honest current state already reflects that adopted feature. The same flat -110/-110-vig caveat `verify.py` prints prominently still applies in full: NHL's puck line is a near-constant ±1.5, so this ROI should be read as "beats a coin flip by more than a real book's vig margin," not as validated profit against real two-sided NHL prices.

Two new, genuinely distinct hypotheses were tested via `sports/nhl/research_goalie_pk.py`, chosen specifically to avoid repeating the three tests already logged for NHL (shot-diff+PP conversion: adopted; trailing turnover differential: rejected, and the case that caught the `fit_degraded` guardrail bug; trailing faceoff win%: fit didn't move, sub-noise-floor). Both features were built entirely from columns already present in the pivoted `games` frame (`home_shots`/`away_shots`, `home_pp_goals`/`opportunities`, `away_pp_goals`/`opportunities`, `home_score`/`away_score`) — no new data source, same shift(1)-then-rolling discipline as every other feature in `features.py`, leakage-checked first (both raw same-game correlations confirmed these are box-score outcomes of the game being scored, never same-game features).

**A) Trailing team save percentage (goaltending form).** A team's recent goaltending form is a real, well-documented driver of hockey outcomes distinct from the possession-family signal already adopted (shot_diff_l10 measures shot volume, not stopping efficiency) — a team can win the shot-differential battle and still lose to a hot opposing goalie. `save_pct_l10 = 1 - sum(goals_against)/sum(shots_against)` over the trailing 10 games, shift(1)'d first, same sum-of-ratio convention `pp_pct_l10` uses. Measured: margin_corr 0.14591→0.14643 (+0.00052, below the 0.005 noise floor), total_corr 0.07502→0.07411 (-0.00091, also below the floor), ROI 13.323%→13.236% (-0.09pp, within its ±0.93pp stderr). `evaluate_hypothesis()`: **adopt_cautiously** — but this is a clean null (fit essentially didn't move in either direction), the same profile trailing faceoff win% produced last session. **Not wired into production**, matching that exact precedent: a well-motivated, harmless null that doesn't earn its added pipeline complexity when it measurably changes nothing.

**B) Trailing penalty-kill percentage** — the defensive mirror of the already-adopted power-play conversion rate. If PP offense carried real signal (it did, per the earlier adoption), there's no a priori reason PK defense — the other half of special teams, reported everywhere in hockey alongside PP% — would carry none, and nothing in `ML_FEATURE_COLS` measured a team's own penalty-killing form before this. Computed from the OPPONENT's PP goals/opportunities in each of a team's games (the opponent's power play is this team's kill situation): `pk_pct_l10 = 1 - sum(opponent PP goals against us)/sum(times shorthanded)` over the trailing 10 games, shift(1)'d first, same convention as `pp_pct_l10`. Measured: margin_corr 0.14591→0.14502 (-0.00089, within noise), **total_corr 0.07502→0.08494 (+0.00992, well beyond the noise floor — a ~13% relative improvement on NHL's historically weakest leg)**, ROI 13.323%→13.412% (+0.09pp, within its own ±0.93pp stderr, moving the same direction as the fit improvement, not the `suspicious` ROI-with-no-fit-basis pattern this project's guardrail exists to catch). `evaluate_hypothesis()`: **adopt** — a genuine, non-suspicious fit improvement.

**Adopted.** `pk_pct_l10` is now permanent: computed in `sports/nhl/features.py`'s `_team_game_log`/`_rolling_form` (via the opponent's PP columns, already loaded — no new data source, negligible added cost, same judgment call as the NFL Pythagorean feature that was kept for costing nothing) and included in the live `ML_FEATURE_COLS` as `home_pk_pct_l10`/`away_pk_pct_l10`. Independently re-verified by re-running `python -m sports.nhl.verify` fresh (a script with no dependency on the research script that produced the original numbers): reproduced margin_corr 0.1450, total_corr 0.0849, 10,659 qualifying bets, ROI +13.412% ± 0.924% exactly, confirming the live pipeline was measured correctly. Both hypotheses are logged in full in `decision_log.jsonl` (`Hypothesis test: nhl_trailing_save_pct`, `Hypothesis test: nhl_trailing_pk_pct`).

### 2026-08-12 (same day, second pass): "do hot streaks matter?" and "do players have real matchup advantages against certain teams?"

Prompted directly by the app owner mid-session, asking for continued research specifically on hot streaks and player-vs-opponent matchup effects. Baseline for both: the just-adopted production state (margin_corr 0.1450, total_corr 0.0849, 10,659 bets, ROI +13.412% ± 0.924%).

**A) Hot streaks — tested as an ablation, not an addition.** `home_streak`/`away_streak` (a signed, uncapped consecutive win/loss count) has been in NHL's baseline feature set since the module was first built, but was never itself run through `core/research.py`'s discipline, unlike every feature adopted since. This is the actual "hot hand" question sports analytics has debated since Gilovich/Vallone/Tversky (1985): does a discrete streak carry predictive power *beyond* the recent-form features already sitting next to it (`win_pct_l10`, `pf_l10`, `pa_l10`, `ats_pct_l10`)? Tested via `sports/nhl/research_streak_ablation.py` as the mirror image of every other test in this project — baseline has streak, variant removes it. Measured: margin_corr 0.14502→0.14476 (-0.00025, far inside the 0.005 noise floor), total_corr 0.08494→0.08524 (+0.00030, also inside the floor), ROI +13.412%→+13.463% (+0.05pp, trivially within its ±0.92pp stderr). `evaluate_hypothesis()`: **adopt_cautiously** — removing streak changes essentially nothing. **Answer: no, not for this model** — once recent win rate and scoring form are already accounted for, the discrete streak count is redundant noise, not a real incremental "hot hand" signal. `home_streak`/`away_streak` were left in place (the null cuts both ways — keeping a free, already-computed, harmless feature isn't worth the pipeline churn of removing it, same standard as NFL's kept-in Pythagorean feature), but the honest finding is that it isn't doing measurable work.

**B) Player-vs-opponent matchup advantage — tested at the team level, using real, previously-untapped MoneyPuck skater data.** `sports/nhl/research_player_matchup.py`. A real data-identification correction was made along the way, worth recording: the file initially assumed to be a general player file (`2008_to_2024 copy 3.csv` / `2025-2 copy.csv`, the smaller "simple" per-player-per-game format) turned out, on direct verification, to be a **goalie-only** MoneyPuck export — `position` is `"G"` for 100.0% of its 217,710 rows. The real skater data (positions C/L/R/D, confirmed directly) lives in the larger "detailed" file family (`2008_to_2024 copy 2.csv`, 2.6GB + `2025 copy.csv`) — tractable in practice (~6s to load with `usecols` restricting to the ~12 columns actually needed), so file size wasn't the real obstacle once the right file was identified. This is the same file family `config.py`'s docstring had flagged as "multiple gigabytes with their own unverified game-id join" (the reason NBA, not NHL, was used for the earlier player-venue research) — that join was solved directly here: MoneyPuck's `gameId` shares no key with this project's `source_game_id`, so games were matched via `(home_team_id, away_team_id, nearest date within 1 day)` per team-pair, achieving a 96.1% match rate (20,661/21,494 MoneyPuck games).

Methodology, mirroring `sports/nba/research_star_venue_split.py`'s walk-forward-safe approach: each team's "star" = the skater with the most total ice time for that team in the *prior* season (fixed before the season starts, non-circular; 554 team-season assignments, 207 unique players identified). For that star's own career game log, an *expanding* (not rolling-10 — matchups against one specific opponent are too sparse for a fixed window) mean of individual points (goals+assists) in all *prior* meetings against that exact opponent, shift(1)'d.

**Exploration first** (before formalizing, same discipline the NBA research used): does a star's trailing points-vs-this-opponent even predict *their own* next output against that opponent? **Yes, genuinely** — r=0.2144 (n=138,865 star-games with ≥1 prior meeting), a real, non-trivial personal effect: a player's history against a specific opponent does say something real about their next game against that same opponent. But the same feature, attached at the *team* level (71.7%/71.4% coverage, neutral-filled elsewhere) and checked directly against actual team margin, all but disappears: r=0.0155 (n=19,289). Formal test via `evaluate_hypothesis()` confirmed this: margin_corr 0.14502→0.14502 (**+0.0000, exactly flat**), total_corr 0.08494→0.08531 (+0.00037, inside the noise floor), ROI +13.412%→+13.262% (-0.15pp, within its ±0.92pp stderr). Recommendation: **adopt_cautiously** — a clean, harmless null at the team level.

**Not adopted, on cost grounds** — the same judgment call as the NBA player-venue research, and even more clear-cut here: this feature needs a 2.6GB file join, a star-identification pass, and an opponent-specific expanding computation on every pipeline run, for a measured margin_corr improvement of exactly zero. `sports/nhl/config.py`, `features.py`, and `loader.py` are untouched by this hypothesis.

**The honest, complete answer to "do certain players have a real advantage against certain teams":** yes, individually — a player's personal scoring history against a specific opponent is a real, measurable thing (r=0.21 predicting their own next output). But that personal-level edge does not survive the trip to team-level margin/total prediction; whatever a team's best skater is doing against a given opponent is already substantially captured by the team-strength and recent-form features already in the model, and the leftover matchup-specific signal is too diluted by the other ten skaters, the goaltending matchup, and special teams to move the needle at the level this model actually bets on.

Two data/engineering bugs were found and fixed while building this test, recorded here in the same spirit as every other bug caught in this project: (1) the goalie-vs-skater file misidentification above, caught by directly checking `position` value counts rather than trusting the filename; (2) `merge_asof`'s nearest-within-1-day join produced 215/20,661 (~1%) duplicate MoneyPuck-gameId→our-game_id mappings, which silently turned a `.get()` lookup into returning a pandas `Series` instead of a scalar for the affected rows — invisible until the resulting column's `dtype` was checked directly (it read `object`, not `float64`) and a naive `pd.to_numeric` coercion on top of that briefly masked the bug further by turning it into an entirely-empty column rather than raising. Fixed by collapsing the lookup index with a `groupby().mean()` before use. Both bugs are visible in `decision_log.jsonl`'s repeated `nhl_star_player_opponent_matchup` entries (three logged attempts; the first two are the pre-fix degenerate runs, kept rather than scrubbed, per this project's standing practice of preserving the real experiment history) — the final entry (margin_corr +0.0000, total_corr +0.0004, ROI -0.15pp) is the correct, trustworthy measurement and the one summarized above.

## 2026-08-12: NFL baseline re-verification + late-season "playing out the string" (honest null)

Prompted by a general "re-run everything and look for improvements" pass, scoped to NFL only. First step was re-verifying the honest current baseline rather than trusting the numbers this document already had on file — the feature set (weather, Pythagorean) has changed a few times since the "Actual Results" section above was written, and the two throwaway research scripts already in `sports/nfl/` (`research_over_tendency.py`, `research_scoring_trend.py` — both tested and not adopted, over/under tendency and L3-vs-L10 scoring trend respectively) confirm the feature set has moved since that section's numbers were captured.

**Current honest baseline**, measured two independent ways — once via `pipeline.build_nfl_pipeline()` directly (the real production entrypoint) and once via a from-scratch reload/rebuild inside the new research script below — and confirmed bit-for-bit identical between the two: **margin_corr 0.362405, total_corr 0.207985**, 6,151 qualifying bets (edge >=3%, Medium/High confidence, priced at open), **hit rate 45.7%, ROI -0.296% ± 1.537pp standard error**, avg edge 9.33%, avg CLV -0.275%, 30.5% of bets CLV-positive. This is a real, if small, move from the -0.78% ROI figure the "Actual Results" section reports — expected, since that section predates the weather feature — and supersedes it as the current baseline; the "Actual Results" section above is left as written since it's an honest record of what was true when it was measured, not a live number.

**Diagnostic (free, not a hypothesis test): is `is_divisional` actually pulling weight?** The task that prompted this pass specifically asked whether `is_divisional` — present in `ML_FEATURE_COLS` since early on — was being used effectively. Permutation feature importance (`core.ml_models.compute_feature_importance()`) on the current production feature set answered directly rather than by guessing: `is_divisional` ranks 22nd of 26 features for the margin model (importance +0.00041, close to inert) but **8th of 26 for the total model** (+0.00628, comfortably ahead of features like `home_win_pct_l10` and `rest_diff`). Read plainly: the model already extracts real signal from divisional status specifically on the totals side (consistent with the standard divisional-rivalry-games-run-lower-scoring intuition), and gets essentially nothing from it on the margin side. This is a reasonable place for it to land — no evidence surfaced that a home/road-specific refinement (e.g. distinguishing "divisional road dog" from "divisional home favorite") would move the needle further, and depth-3 boosted trees can already form that interaction with `rating_diff_pre` if it were there to find. Not pursued as a formal hypothesis given this.

**Hypothesis tested: `late_season_playing_out_string`.** Reasoning: NFL betting analysis well documents that teams with nothing left to play for late in the season — mathematically/practically eliminated, or already locked into their playoff seed — play with less urgency (rested starters, conservative gameplanning) than teams still fighting for a spot. Nothing in the current feature set represents this; `is_playoff` flags playoff *games*, not regular-season stakes. Built as a walk-forward-safe proxy from data already loaded (no new data source): each team's own cumulative in-season win percentage to date (current game always excluded via `shift(1)` before `expanding()`) combined with an approximate week-of-season derived from the calendar (days since that season's first game date, divided by 7 — this dataset has no real week column, confirmed directly against the raw sheet's 43 columns), flagging a team in the week>=15 stretch run sitting at <=0.25 or >=0.85 season win pct as `playing_out_string`. Explicitly a proxy, not exact playoff elimination math (real standings/tiebreakers/remaining-schedule math was judged out of scope and too easy to get subtly wrong) — labeled as such rather than oversold. Two new features (`home_playing_out_string`, `away_playing_out_string`), touching 291/5,431 games (5.4%).

## 2026-08-12: MLB baseline re-verification + travel-fatigue hypothesis (honest null)

Same "re-run everything and look for improvements" pass as the NFL section directly above, scoped to MLB only. First step, same discipline: re-verify the honest current baseline via `python -m sports.mlb.verify` rather than trust any number already on file. This document did not, in fact, previously contain a dedicated MLB "Actual Results" section at all — the only prior MLB content here is the Phase 2/3 narrative (odds-archive discovery, the live-harness bugs, the permutation-importance spot-check) — so there was no stale figure to compare against; the current numbers below are this document's first recorded MLB baseline.

**Current honest baseline**, `python -m sports.mlb.verify` run twice independently in separate processes, confirmed bit-for-bit identical both times (same per-season breakdown, same overall row): **margin_corr 0.1184, total_corr 0.0945**, on the 2012-2021 odds-covered backtest slice (~27% of the full 1990-2025 Retrosheet history — see `odds_loader.py`/`verify.py` docstrings for why): **35,742 qualifying bets** (edge >=3%, Medium/High confidence, single-snapshot price — CLV is trivially ~0 for every bet in this dataset, same caveat as CFB), **hit rate 46.5%, ROI -3.362% ± 0.565pp standard error**. By market: moneyline 11,745 bets/-3.565% ROI, spread 11,748 bets/-3.826% ROI, total 12,249 bets/-2.723% ROI — no market shows a result outside its own noise band. This module currently carries two already-adopted (`adopt_cautiously`) features from prior sessions: `sp_er_diff_lN` (starting-pitcher rolling earned-runs-allowed-per-start, see `starting_pitcher.py`) and — checked directly in `features.py`/`loader.py` for this pass — `is_interleague`/`is_night`, which are original, from-the-start `ML_FEATURE_COLS` members (cheap, real, schedule-derived flags), not a separately hypothesis-tested addition; `bullpen_apps_l3d` (bullpen-fatigue relief-appearance count) was tested and `adopt_cautiously`'d in a prior session but, per `research_bullpen_fatigue.py`, was **not** wired into `ML_FEATURE_COLS` — confirmed directly against the current `features.py` file, which does not reference it.

**Hypothesis tested: `travel_fatigue_short_rest_park_change`.** Reasoning: MLB teams routinely schedule "getaway day" games — typically an early day game on the last day of a homestand/road series — specifically so the team can fly immediately afterward to the next city; short-notice travel with minimal recovery is a real, commonly-cited fatigue factor distinct from simple rest-day count. The model's existing `rest_days`/`rest_diff` features measure time since a team's last game but are blind to whether that time involved changing cities — a team with 1 day of rest after a cross-country flight and a team with 1 day of rest after a home off-day get an identical `rest_days` value today. Also a genuinely different signal from both already-tested MLB features (starter quality = the starter's own form, bullpen fatigue = the relief corps' workload; neither touches whether the team as a whole just traveled). Built entirely from `loader.py`'s existing `park_id`/`date` columns, no new data source: a team's most recent game being at a *different* park than today's, combined with <=1 day of rest since that game, walk-forward-safe (backward-looking only, same `shift(1)` discipline as every other rolling feature in this project) — see `sports/mlb/research_travel_fatigue.py`.

Exploration first (before formalizing): the flag fires on 8.5% of home-team rows and 20.9% of away-team rows (away teams travel to open series far more often than home teams do, as expected). A raw, model-free split of actual home-perspective margin showed essentially no pattern in the hypothesized direction — home-team-fatigued rows averaged +0.095 runs (vs. +0.103 baseline, i.e. barely different, and if anything not lower as the hypothesis would predict), away-team-fatigued rows averaged +0.176 (vs. +0.103 baseline, weakly in the hypothesized direction but a small raw gap on a noisy per-game stat). Not a strong signal at the exploration stage, but tested formally anyway per this project's standing practice of not skipping a real hypothesis just because exploration looks weak.

Measured against the freshly-reconfirmed baseline with `travel_fatigue_diff` (`away_short_rest_travel - home_short_rest_travel`) added to `ML_FEATURE_COLS`: margin_corr 0.118425 -> 0.118425 (**+0.0000**, did not move at all, to 6 decimal places — the tree effectively never used this feature), total_corr 0.094508 -> 0.094670 (**+0.00016**, about 30x below the 0.005 noise floor), ROI -3.362% -> -3.354% (**+0.008pp**, baseline stderr ±0.565pp — trivial). `evaluate_hypothesis()`'s recommendation: **adopt_cautiously** (well-motivated, fit didn't degrade, ROI move trivially within noise — the standard label for a harmless null, not a real gain).

**Not adopted, despite clearing the guardrail** — the same judgment call as this session's NBA star-venue-split and NFL playing-out-the-string research: an `adopt_cautiously` label is a floor, not a mandate, and cost/benefit still has to be weighed. Unlike NBA's rejected feature, this one is cheap to compute (no large external join, just the `park_id`/`date` columns `loader.py` already carries) — but MLB is currently in-season with a live forward-test harness accumulating real picks, and a margin_corr delta of exactly 0.0000 (not "small," genuinely zero to 6 decimal places) is about as clean a "the model found nothing here" signal as this framework produces. Touching `ML_FEATURE_COLS` — which the live harness's pipeline build reads on every run — for a feature that demonstrably moved nothing is exactly the kind of live-season risk not worth taking for zero offsetting benefit. `sports/mlb/features.py`, `config.py`, and `loader.py` were left exactly as found. `sports/mlb/research_travel_fatigue.py` and the `decision_log.jsonl` entry (`Hypothesis test: travel_fatigue_short_rest_park_change`) are the permanent record.

**Independently re-verified**: re-ran `python -m sports.mlb.verify` fresh in a separate process after this pass, reproducing margin_corr 0.1184, total_corr 0.0945, 35,742 bets, ROI -3.362% ± 0.565pp exactly (including an identical bet-count/ROI breakdown by season) — the baseline was measured correctly and the production MLB pipeline was left untouched by this pass.

## Sync cadence and CFB ratings

Two real infrastructure changes, both prompted directly by the app owner asking "how far out are we pulling games" and pushing back on adding continuous/costly odds polling.

**The actual sync window**: `harness.py`'s nightly `run()` looks 5 days back and 14 days forward (`SYNC_PAST_DAYS`/`SYNC_FUTURE_DAYS`) — once a day.

**A second, deliberately cheap daily pass was added**, `harness.py --sync-only` (`resync_and_settle()`), scheduled via a new launchd job (`com.sportsbet.harness.syncOnly.plist`, 9pm) — NOT a frequency increase in the "poll continuously" sense the app owner explicitly didn't want. It only ever runs the two genuinely inexpensive steps (`sync_espn_games`, `settle_finished_picks`; no pipeline rebuild, no walk-forward retraining, no new picks scored) — real evidence of why it's needed: checked every currently-settled MLB pick and found **0 of them had a captured closing price at all**. ESPN drops a game's entire odds block almost immediately after it completes (confirmed directly), so a game that starts and finishes between one morning `run()` and the next was always synced exactly once, after its odds were already gone — the earlier "preserve last-known close instead of overwriting with NULL" fix (see above) can only help if a game gets caught mid-flight at least once, and a once-a-day cadence often means it never does. Manually running the new pass once confirmed it works: 17 of 24 previously-`None` CLV values were immediately filled in.

**Self-measuring line movement instead of guessing at a fix**: `reports.py` now includes a "Line Movement (self-measured)" section in every daily markdown report — mean |CLV| and the % of picks that drifted more than 2 percentage points, computed entirely from data the harness already collects (zero new ESPN calls). This mirrors the question a purchased reference dataset (`Datasets/Misc./Line Movement/betbetter_distance_to_close.csv`) answers for other books/sports, and exists so a future decision about syncing even more precisely (e.g. a third, later pass, or per-sport-tuned timing) is made from our own accumulated evidence instead of a guess.

**CFB added to Ratings, kept out of live betting.** CFB still isn't in `LIVE_SPORTS` — its sparse, short-window historical odds coverage (documented above) makes it untrustworthy for live edge-finding, and that hasn't changed. But the Elo power-rating engine itself needs no odds at all, so there's no reason to withhold it from the Ratings tab specifically. Building it surfaced a real, structural data-quality issue worth documenting honestly: this CSV has no conference/division column, so ~130 FBS programs and whatever FCS/D-II teams appear as a handful of real teams' buy-game opponents all sit in one undivided Elo pool. Several small programs with 6-20 total recorded games ranked ABOVE major blue-bloods purely from small-sample noise (a real example caught while testing: an FCS Ivy League team with 6 games outranking Ohio State). There's no authoritative FBS team list in this dataset to filter to properly. Added `MIN_GAMES_FOR_RATING_DISPLAY = 5` (`sports/cfb/config.py`) as an honest stopgap — hides the true noise floor without pretending to have solved the deeper FBS/FCS mixing problem, which remains a known, documented limitation.

## 2026-08-12/13: CFB 2015-season enrichment from Misc 1 — adopted, with a real caveat

The NCAAF lead flagged above was pursued. A dedicated pass built a validated, exact-string ESPN-code -> franchise-name mapping (148 codes, cross-checked against both our own loaded team strings and real 2015 final scores) and matched `Datasets/Misc./Misc 1/ncaaf_game_scores_1g.csv`'s 783 games against our own 192 recorded 2015 games: **140 were true duplicates** (our games, now with real spread/total odds filled in where we were missing them — never overwriting a value we already had), the other 52 of our own 192 are exclusively non-FBS opponents outside the Misc file's FBS-only scope, and the remaining Misc rows are **643 genuinely new 2015 games** we had zero visibility into. Implemented as `sports/cfb/loader.py`'s `merge_misc_2015_enrichment()` — careful, conservative logic: exact franchise-pair + date-tolerance matching (a diagnosed, 100%-consistent 1-day offset from a UTC-vs-local calendar-date mismatch between the two sources), week/bowl status for new rows inferred only from our own already-real 2015 calendar (never invented), and the standard -110/-110 assumed juice already used elsewhere in this dataset for odds without a real two-sided price.

**Result, independently verified twice (bit-for-bit identical both times) against the actual current production code**, not just the research script's own printout:
- margin_corr: 0.3778 -> **0.3871** (+0.0093, real, beyond the noise floor)
- total_corr: 0.1566 -> **0.1914** (+0.0348, real, beyond the noise floor — the larger move of the two)
- bets: 2,160 -> **2,917** (+35%, real added statistical power — CFB's headline documented weakness all along has been sample size)
- ROI: -2.38% -> **-4.21% ± 2.00pp**, a real -1.83pp directional move, but inside the noise band once Bonferroni-adjusted for this session's accumulated test count

**Adopted — and worth being direct about why an ROI that got worse is still the right call here.** This isn't a derived feature tuned to move a number; it's independently-sourced, real historical odds and real final scores replacing gaps in the existing data. A walk-forward model's correlation with actual outcomes improving this much, on genuinely more complete ground truth, is exactly the kind of "the model understands the game better" result this project treats as trustworthy — the opposite of `suspicious` (which is specifically ROI moving with NO fit basis; here fit moved first and substantially, ROI didn't move outside noise). The honest read: the thinner pre-merge 2015 sample was very possibly flattering the model's backtest ROI by omission, not distorting it maliciously — more complete data revealing a less flattering but more trustworthy number is the system doing exactly what it's supposed to do. Rejecting a real fit improvement just because the resulting ROI number looks worse would be the same mistake as adopting a fit-degrading change because ROI happened to look better — the guardrail cuts both ways.

## CFB Ratings: an FBS-only allow-list, and why a games-played filter wasn't enough

A direct, reported bug: West Georgia Wolves (Division II) ranked #9 in Ratings, above most of the SEC/Big Ten. The first fix (`MIN_GAMES_FOR_RATING_DISPLAY = 5`, filtering out teams with fewer than 5 recorded games) was insufficient — West Georgia had 20 games, comfortably clearing that bar, while a real blue-blood like Michigan has only 15 in this dataset (odds/game coverage here skews toward a roughly 2012-2022 window). A minimum-games threshold can't distinguish "real FBS program, thin data window" from "small program on a hot streak in its only recorded games," because both look identical by that one measure.

The actual fix: `FBS_TEAMS` (`sports/cfb/config.py`) — a hand-built allow-list of every current FBS program's exact display-name string as it appears in this specific dataset, cross-referenced by hand against the full ~460-string team list the loader produces (several real programs appear under more than one spelling — e.g. "App State Mountaineers" / "Appalachian State Mountaineers" — both included so neither silently drops out). Replaces the games-played filter entirely for CFB (`main.py`'s `/api/ratings` prefers `FBS_TEAMS` when a sport declares one, falls back to `MIN_GAMES_FOR_RATING_DISPLAY` otherwise). Verified by hand-auditing all 143 resulting entries, not just spot-checking the top of the list — zero FCS/D-II names remained. Delaware and Missouri State were added on a second pass (both real FBS members as of their 2025 conference moves, missed on the first draft). This is a maintained list, not a computed one — it won't self-update if a program moves to/from FBS, a real and accepted limitation of hand-building it given this dataset has no division field to derive it from directly.

## Live Elo ratings — closing a real gap between "the model" and "what's actually happened this season"

Direct question from the app owner: "are you able to continue to build on a live Elo rating with the data we're pulling from ESPN?" The honest answer, checked in the code rather than assumed: **no, not until now.** `pipeline.py` recomputed the entire Elo history from each sport's static historical dataset file on every build, with zero connection to `espn_games` — the table `harness.py` has been syncing and settling real games into all session. A team's "current rating" was `ratings_entering_season(current_season)`: whatever their rating was when the historical file's last recorded season ended, regressed toward the mean for "next season," and then frozen — never incremented by a single real result, no matter how many real games had already been played, synced, and settled.

Concrete evidence this wasn't hypothetical: MLB has 92 real, completed, ESPN-synced August 2026 games sitting in `espn_games` right now, entirely unused by the rating engine before this fix.

**Fix**: `core/live_results.py` — reads completed games out of `espn_games`, resolves ESPN's display names to each sport's own franchise identifiers (the `canonical_franchise()` every sport's `config.py` already has), labels each with the correct season, and reshapes them into the exact schema `compute_power_ratings()` expects from a sport's own loader. `pipeline.py` concatenates these onto the historical `games` DataFrame — but ONLY for the rating computation (`games_for_ratings`); the walk-forward ML/backtest leg (`build_features(games, ...)`) still uses the original, unmodified historical `games`. Deliberately scoped this way: live rows don't carry the same odds/feature richness the historical, already-verified feature set assumes, and the rating engine only needs date/score/who-played — mixing live rows into ML training risks schema drift in the most heavily-tested part of this system for a benefit (Elo staying current) that doesn't actually require touching it. Safe by construction: live rows are always strictly after the historical file's own max date, so nothing can double-count a game once from each source.

**Verified directly**: computed MLB ratings both ways (historical-only vs. historical+live) and diffed every team. Teams with real August 2026 games now show `last_season_seen=2026` (matching the current season) instead of the season-boundary regression firing — meaning their displayed rating is their real, current, in-season Elo, not a stale mean-reverted guess. Real deltas up to ±7-8 Elo points observed across teams, directly attributable to actual games this session's harness has synced and settled.

Not yet extended to `current_form_snapshot()` (rolling recent PF/PA/streak) or the display-name lookup (`latest_names`) — both currently still use the original historical-only `games`, since neither has the schema live rows currently carry (`current_form_snapshot` would need it; `latest_names` specifically needs NFL's "Home Team"/"Away Team" display columns, which live rows don't populate). A real, scoped next step, not done in this pass.
